How to File a Water Damage Insurance Claim in Idaho

The short answer

The short answer

Filing a water damage claim in Idaho is a documentation job before it is a repair job. Stop the water, photograph everything before anything moves, call your insurer promptly, and then build the paper trail — the claim number, the adjuster’s name, a detailed inventory, every receipt, and a written scope of the work — because the settlement is decided from that paper trail, not from how bad the damage looked. The two mistakes that cost people the most are doing permanent repairs before the adjuster has seen the damage, and accepting the first settlement number without checking it against the written scope.

Whether the damage is covered at all — sudden burst pipe versus slow leak, flood water versus a backup — is a separate question, and it is answered in the companion guide: Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold in Idaho?. Read that first if you have not already, because the cause decides the claim. This post assumes the cause is a covered one and walks through the filing itself, step by step.

If the water is still coming in, or the wet area is bigger than a small patch, call (321) 985-3339 and describe where the water came from and how big the wet area looks. An honest answer starts with what needs to be looked at, not with a price.

Water still coming in? Stop the water first, photograph everything, then call your insurer — the photos you take in the first hour are the evidence the claim runs on.

The first hour

Before you call: the first hour decides the claim

The Idaho Department of Insurance gives the order of operations, and it is worth following exactly, because every step after it depends on what you do here.

1. Stop the water and make the area safe. Shut off the supply line or the appliance that failed, or the main supply if you cannot tell where the water is coming from. Stay away from standing water near outlets, cords, and the breaker panel. Do not wait for the adjuster to do this — your policy requires you to mitigate, and mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours.

2. Photograph and video everything before anything moves. Wide shots of each room first, then close-ups of the damage, the source of the water, and anything you will have to throw away. The Idaho Department of Insurance says to do this before mitigation work starts if you can, and not to throw anything out until your adjuster tells you it is all right — adjusters like to inspect damaged items themselves.

3. Mitigate, but do not rebuild. Get the water out and the area drying. Emergency work to stop further damage is expected of you, and the receipts for it belong in the claim. Permanent repairs are different: do not tear out and rebuild before the adjuster has seen the damage, because a claim can be denied if the evidence is gone before inspection.

4. Write down the timeline while it is fresh. When you first noticed the water, what you believe caused it, what you did to stop it, when you called for help. A month later, reconstructed from memory, this is the part of the claim that goes vague — and vague is what gets questioned.

Step 1

Report the claim, and write down what they tell you

Call your insurer’s claims line, or use its app or website, as soon as you can — ideally the same day. Most policies require you to report water damage within 30 days, and sooner is always safer. If you cannot reach the insurer, the Idaho Department of Insurance suggests calling your agent.

When you call, stick to the facts: what happened, when you discovered it, and what you have done to prevent further damage. Then write down three things before you hang up:

  • Your claim number.
  • Your adjuster’s name and contact information.
  • What they need from you next, and whether they want to inspect before certain materials are removed.

The date you found the water and what caused it is the claim. If the cause is disputed later, the photos, the plumber’s invoice, and the timeline from the first hour are the evidence that settles it — which is why the first hour matters more than the phone call.

Step 2

The adjuster visit — what to have ready

The adjuster is not there to decide whether you deserve help. They are there to measure the scope of the loss and match it against the policy. The visit goes better, and the scope comes out more accurate, when the evidence is laid out for them instead of described from memory.

Have these ready before the visit:

  • The photos and video from before anything moved, on a phone or printed, room by room.
  • The inventory list, started, not finished. Brand, model, serial number, measurements, and what you paid, for every damaged item — the Idaho Department of Insurance asks for exactly this detail. Start it while the damage is still visible; items reconstructed a month later are the ones that get left off.
  • Every receipt so far. The plumber’s bill, water extraction, drying equipment, fans you bought, hotel nights if the home is unlivable. If it is not documented, it is not in the claim.
  • The failed part, if you kept it. A burst hose, a broken fitting, a failed valve. Keep it until the adjuster says otherwise.
  • The written scope from the remediation company, if you have one — what comes out, what gets dried, what gets tested. The scope is what the adjuster approves and what the insurer pays against. A verbal estimate is not a scope.

During the visit, ask the adjuster to confirm in writing what is covered before you approve repairs. Read any contractor contract carefully before signing it. And do not agree to permanent work starting before the inspection unless it is emergency mitigation — the distinction matters, and it is the one adjusters ask about first.

Step 3

How the payment is actually calculated

This is the part of the process most homeowners only learn about when the first cheque arrives smaller than expected. Ask your adjuster these two questions early, in writing, so the numbers are not a surprise:

Do I have replacement cost or actual cash value coverage? With replacement cost coverage, insurers often pay the actual cash value first and hold back the depreciation, paying the rest after the repairs are done and documented. With actual cash value coverage, the depreciation is not paid back at all. The difference between the two is often the largest gap in the whole settlement, and it is decided by the policy, not by negotiation.

What is my deductible, and what counts against it? The deductible comes off the top of the covered loss. Emergency mitigation, drying, and the remediation itself usually count toward the covered loss; the failed pipe or appliance itself usually does not — the policy pays for the damage the water caused, not for the thing that broke.

On the mold side specifically, the numbers from blog #7 still apply: a typical mold sublimit runs $1,000 to $10,000, minus the deductible, and a burst pipe with a $5,000 mold sublimit usually leaves you paying only the deductible, while the same mold from a slow leak leaves you paying the whole job. The national published figures for the job itself are most professional mold remediation jobs between $1,200 and $3,750, averaging around $2,300, usually priced at $10 to $25 per square foot of affected area (HomeAdvisor, June 2026; This Old House, March 2026). There is no published Boise-specific price list for these jobs, and none is claimed here.

Step 4

The written scope is the claim

Once the adjuster has visited, the claim turns into a comparison of two documents: the insurer’s approved scope and the remediation company’s written scope. The gap between them is where claims are won or lost, and it is a negotiation, not a verdict.

A written scope should name, for each affected area: what comes out, what gets dried in place, what gets cleaned and kept, what gets tested, and what the drying and clearance check look like. Moisture readings belong in it — they are the objective evidence that shows where the water travelled, which is what makes the scope credible to an adjuster. If the insurer’s scope is smaller than the remediation company’s scope, ask for the difference to be explained item by item, and get a second written scope if the gap is large. A second scope from another remediator is evidence, not an argument.

Do not sign a repair contract that starts permanent work before the scope is agreed. And keep every version of the scope, every email, and every receipt in one place — the claim file you build is the claim.

Step 5

Reading the settlement — and the supplemental claim

When the settlement arrives, check it against the written scope line by line, not against the total. Three things to look for:

  • Missing items. Rooms, materials, or belongings that were in the inventory but not in the settlement. The inventory list from Step 2 is the checklist.
  • Depreciation held back. If you have replacement cost coverage, the held-back depreciation should be recoverable once repairs are complete and documented with receipts and photos. Ask what documentation releases it, and by when.
  • Work found after the walls open. Water damage jobs routinely grow once drywall comes out — wet insulation, a second wet room, framing that needs drying. That extra work is a supplemental claim: document it the same way as the original loss (photos before removal, moisture readings, written scope), and submit it before the work is covered up. A supplement submitted with photos and a scope is a routine part of the process; one submitted after the rebuild is a fight.

If the settlement is short and the shortfall is a scope gap, the answer is more documentation, not a louder phone call. Send the adjuster the missing photos, the second scope, the moisture readings, and ask for the specific items to be reconsidered, in writing.

If denied

If the claim is denied

A denial is not the end of it, but it has to be answered on the policy’s terms. The full denial playbook — get the denial in writing citing the policy section, check the cause finding against your proof, check the endorsement page for a mold exclusion added at renewal, get a second scope — is in blog #7, and it applies here word for word, because most denials turn on the same cause question.

Two Idaho-specific levers from that guide are worth repeating here, because they are the ones homeowners do not know about:

  • The Idaho Department of Insurance takes homeowner complaints and questions about a claim. It cannot adjust your claim or order payment, but it takes the calls — (208) 334-4250 in the Boise area, 1-800-721-3272 statewide.
  • Idaho Code 41-1839. If an insurer fails to pay the amount justly due within 30 days after proof of loss is furnished, and you have to sue or go to arbitration, the court can award reasonable attorney’s fees. That is the reason the proof of loss — the inventory, the receipts, the written scope, all submitted and dated — matters as much as the damage itself.

Conclusion

Filing a water damage insurance claim in Idaho comes down to four things: document before you move anything, report promptly and write down the claim number and the adjuster’s name, get the scope in writing before permanent work starts, and check the settlement against that scope line by line — including whether your coverage is replacement cost or actual cash value, and what documentation releases the held-back depreciation. The cause decides whether the claim is covered at all, and that question is answered in the companion insurance guide; the paper trail decides how much of it gets paid. Questions

Common questions

How do I file a water damage insurance claim in Idaho?

Stop the water, photograph and video everything before anything moves, mitigate so the damage does not spread, then call your insurer promptly and write down the claim number and the adjuster’s name. Build the inventory list, keep every receipt, and get the remediation scope in writing before permanent repairs start.

What should I have ready for the adjuster’s visit?

Photos and video from before anything moved, a started inventory list with brand, model, serial number and measurements, every receipt so far, the failed part if you kept it, and the remediation company’s written scope. Do not throw anything out until the adjuster says it is all right.

How does the insurer decide what to pay?

From the written scope matched against the policy. With replacement cost coverage, the insurer often pays actual cash value first and the depreciation after repairs are done and documented; with actual cash value coverage, the depreciation is not paid back. Ask which one you have, in writing, before repairs start.

Can I start repairs before the adjuster visits?

Emergency mitigation, yes — your policy requires it. Permanent repairs, no: do not tear out and rebuild before the adjuster has seen the damage, because a claim can be denied if the evidence is gone before inspection.

What if the settlement is too low?

Check it against the written scope line by line, looking for missing items and held-back depreciation. Send the adjuster the missing documentation — photos, a second scope, moisture readings — and ask for the specific items to be reconsidered in writing. Extra work found after walls open is a supplemental claim, submitted with photos and a scope before it is covered up.

What if my claim is denied?

Ask for the denial in writing, citing the policy section, and check the cause finding against your proof — a plumber’s invoice dated the day it happened, photos of the burst. The Idaho Department of Insurance takes homeowner complaints about claims at (208) 334-4250 in Boise or 1-800-721-3272 statewide, and Idaho Code 41-1839 can award attorney’s fees if the insurer fails to pay what is justly due within 30 days after proof of loss and you have to sue or arbitrate. The full denial steps are in the companion insurance guide.

Does homeowners insurance cover mold from water damage in Idaho?

It depends on the cause: sudden, accidental water damage is often covered, while slow leaks, neglect, and flood water usually are not, and many policies cap mold coverage. See the full Idaho insurance guide.
