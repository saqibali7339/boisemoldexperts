Scenario

A typical scenario

Illustrative example, not a record of a past job.

A family in a large Eagle home with a finished basement notices the basement bathroom floor is wet and the room smells. The upstairs toilets work normally. The homeowner stops using the basement fixtures, keeps the kids upstairs and photographs the floor. The pattern points to the ejector pump. A plumber replaces the failed pump, and a cleanup crew removes the sewage from the floor, takes out the affected carpet and pad, disinfects the area and dries the room.

Who comes first

Plumber, cleanup crew or sewer provider: who comes first?

  1. Stop the source. If the line is still blocked or the pump has failed, more sewage will keep coming. A plumber clears or repairs it. If the problem looks like the city’s main, call the Eagle Sewer District at (208) 939-0132.

  2. Contain and remove the sewage. A cleanup crew removes standing sewage and affected materials. You can call for cleanup at the same time as the plumber. You don’t have to wait.

  3. Report to your insurer. Photograph everything before it is cleaned, keep receipts, and ask whether you have sewer or water backup coverage.

The cleanup

What sewage cleanup involves

Sewage is the most contaminated water a home cleanup deals with. Industry guidance (ANSI/IICRC S500) classes sewage as Category 3 water, grossly contaminated, so it is handled differently from clean water. CDC guidance says to keep children and pets out of the affected area until cleanup is complete, to wear rubber boots, gloves and goggles during cleanup, and to remove drywall and insulation that sewage has touched.

Remove and contain

Standing sewage is pumped out. The affected area is separated so the contamination doesn’t spread through the house.

Discard what is porous

Carpet, pad, drywall, insulation and similar materials that soaked up sewage are generally removed and discarded, not cleaned.

Clean and disinfect

Hard surfaces and framing that can be kept are cleaned and disinfected.

Dry and verify

Air movers and dehumidifiers run until readings are back to normal. For more on drying, see the water damage timeline.

Insurance

Is a sewage backup covered?

Often not by default. The Insurance Information Institute notes that sewer backups are not covered by a standard homeowner policy, and not by flood insurance either. Backup coverage has to be added as an endorsement, and the Institute puts the cost at roughly $40 to $50 a year. The pipe running from your house to the street main is usually your responsibility to repair, separate from the damage inside the home. Read your policy, and call your insurer early. This is general information and not a coverage opinion.

Questions

Common questions

Why did only my basement bathroom back up?

In many homes with a basement bathroom, waste is pumped up to the main line by an ejector pump. If that pump fails or its discharge is blocked, only the basement fixtures back up while the rest of the house works. A plumber can confirm it.

Can I keep using the upstairs bathrooms?

Not until the cause is found. Every flush and every load of laundry sends more water into a line that is already blocked, and it can push more waste out below.

Is it safe to stay in the house?

Often yes, if the affected area is separated from the rest of the home. Keep children and pets out, don’t walk through it and then through clean rooms, and wash your hands well. If a large area is affected or anyone is unwell, ask a professional first.

Can I clean it up myself?

A small spill from a single fixture can sometimes be handled with proper protection, but porous materials that soaked it up usually can’t be cleaned. Anything larger than a small area is better left to a crew.
